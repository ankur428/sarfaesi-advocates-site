Few judgments are cited as often in SARFAESI disputes as *Mardia Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311. Borrowers, banks and tribunals still refer to it, so it helps to understand what it actually says.

## Why the case arose

The SARFAESI Act 2002 allowed secured creditors to enforce security without going to court. Borrowers challenged the law itself, arguing that it gave lenders strong powers without enough safeguards. The Supreme Court examined those challenges in *Mardia Chemicals*.

## What the Supreme Court recognised

In broad terms, the Court:

- upheld the **validity** of the SARFAESI Act as a measure to help lenders recover dues
- accepted that a borrower must have a **fair opportunity to put forward objections** once a Section 13(2) notice is received
- indicated that the secured creditor should **consider those objections and reply with reasons**, rather than ignore them

The Act has been amended since the judgment, so **check the current text of the Act** for the present position on each provision.

## What this means for a borrower

The idea that a reply must be reasoned is a practical one. If you receive a Section 13(2) notice, you can:

1. **Read the notice closely**, including the amount claimed, the assets listed and the date of default.
2. **Send a written objection** within the time stated in the notice, pointing to specific errors or disputes.
3. **Keep proof** that the objection was delivered.
4. **Examine the bank's reply** to see whether it actually deals with your points.

A reply that merely repeats the demand without engaging with the objections may be something to raise in later proceedings.

## What the case does not do

*Mardia Chemicals* is not a guarantee that any objection will succeed, and it does not stop a bank from proceeding once it has replied. It also does not remove the need to follow the remedies the Act provides. Later, in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged writ petitions against SARFAESI action where an effective remedy exists. A borrower should therefore look first at the **Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal**, within the limitation period in the Act.

## Working alongside other decisions

The judgment is one piece of a larger picture. In *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) 1 SCC 125, the Court explained that SARFAESI and the Recovery of Debts Act operate together. In *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610, it stressed that Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 must be complied with.

## Practical points

- Treat the **notice period** as a firm deadline.
- Keep your **loan statement, notice and correspondence** together in date order.
- Make objections **specific and in writing**.
- Note every date after the notice, including possession and sale notices.

## Getting advice

Case law helps only when applied to your own documents and dates. A careful review of the notice, your loan account and the bank's reply is the best starting point.
