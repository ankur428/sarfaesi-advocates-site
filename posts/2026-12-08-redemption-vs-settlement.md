Redemption vs settlement is a choice between paying the bank everything it is owed and agreeing with the bank to close the account on different terms. Under the SARFAESI Act 2002, redemption is a right the borrower holds by law, while a settlement exists only if the bank agrees to it. This article explains how the two differ and what to weigh before choosing.

## What is redemption under the SARFAESI Act?

Redemption means getting the secured asset back by paying the secured creditor its dues. Section 13(8) of the Act deals with this: if the borrower tenders all the dues, together with the costs and expenses the bank has incurred, within the time the section allows, the bank cannot go ahead with the sale or transfer of the asset. For the full detail, see our post on the [right of redemption under Section 13(8)](/blog/2026-10-07-right-of-redemption-section-13-8).

Two points matter in practice. First, the right is tied to timing, so the date by which payment must be tendered needs to be checked against the current text of the Act and the sale notice. Second, redemption means the whole amount, not a part of it.

## What is a one time settlement?

A one time settlement is an agreement, not a statutory right. The borrower proposes a sum, the bank considers it under its own policy, and if the bank sanctions it, the account is closed on those terms. The amount may be less than the full dues, but only if the bank agrees.

Because it rests on agreement, the bank can decline, counter-offer or set conditions. Our post on [one time settlement under SARFAESI](/blog/2026-10-09-one-time-settlement-sarfaesi) covers how these proposals work.

## How do the two routes differ?

The main differences are these:

- Redemption is a right under the Act; settlement depends on the bank's consent.
- Redemption requires payment of all dues and costs; settlement may be for a negotiated amount.
- Redemption is bound by the statutory timing; settlement follows the dates in the bank's sanction letter.
- Redemption stops the sale of the asset; a settlement stops it only if its terms say so and the payments are made on time.

## Which route fits your situation?

If you can arrange the full dues and costs in time, redemption gives certainty, because the outcome does not turn on the bank's discretion. If the full amount is out of reach but you can pay a lump sum or a defined set of instalments, a settlement may be worth proposing.

Many borrowers do both in sequence: they raise funds, approach the bank for a settlement, and keep redemption in mind as the fallback if the bank refuses. What you should not do is let the sale date pass while waiting for a reply. A settlement discussion does not by itself stop enforcement, so protect your position in writing and keep an eye on the sale timeline.

If a challenge is already pending, the route also interacts with it. Read about [settling while a Section 17 application is pending](/blog/2026-11-19-section-17-settlement-while-pending), and see our page on [one-time settlement and redemption](/one-time-settlement-redemption) for the wider picture.

## What should you get in writing?

Whichever route you take, insist on a written record. For a settlement, that means a signed sanction letter with the amount, dates and consequences of default. For redemption, keep proof of the tender and of the payment, and ask for written confirmation that the sale will not proceed and the security will be released.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I redeem the property after the auction has taken place?
The right under Section 13(8) is linked to the stage of the sale, so it is not safe to assume it survives a completed sale. Check the current text of the Act and the date fixed for sale as early as possible.

### Does applying for a settlement stop the auction?
No. A proposal alone does not stop the bank from continuing with enforcement. Unless the bank agrees in writing to hold the sale, steps under the notice may go on.

### Can the bank refuse a one time settlement?
Yes. A settlement needs the bank's consent, and the bank may decline or propose different terms. Redemption, by contrast, depends on paying the dues and costs rather than on the bank's agreement.
