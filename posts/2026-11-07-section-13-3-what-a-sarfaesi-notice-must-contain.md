A notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act is the first formal step before a bank can enforce its security. Section 13(3) deals with what that notice must say. A notice that leaves out key details can be questioned, so it is worth reading yours line by line. As the provisions have been amended over time, always check the current text of the Act.

## What Section 13(3) requires

In broad terms, the notice must give details of the amount payable by the borrower and the secured assets the creditor intends to enforce if the borrower does not pay. The notice is addressed to the borrower and calls on the borrower to discharge the full liability within the period stated in Section 13(2).

## A practical checklist

When you receive a notice, check whether it:

- Names the correct borrower and, where relevant, the guarantors.
- States the amount claimed and shows how it is made up (principal, interest and other charges).
- Identifies the loan account and the facility to which the claim relates.
- Describes the secured assets with enough detail to identify them, such as the property description or the hypothecated goods.
- Records the date on which the account was classified as a non-performing asset.
- States the consequence of non-payment, namely that the creditor may proceed under Section 13(4).

## Why the details matter

The notice sets the framework for everything that follows. If the amount is unclear or the asset is wrongly described, the borrower may find it hard to reply meaningfully, and later steps such as possession and sale may be open to challenge. In *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610, the Supreme Court stressed that the Rules, including Rules 8 and 9, must be complied with. Compliance at each stage is part of a lawful enforcement.

## Your right to respond

The borrower can send a written reply raising objections. In *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311, the Supreme Court held that a borrower can object to the notice and the creditor must reply with reasons. Keep a copy of your reply, proof of delivery and the bank's response.

## If you disagree with the bank

If the bank proceeds despite your objections, a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal may be considered, within the time stated in the Act. Writ petitions are not usually the first step; in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged writs where an effective statutory remedy exists.

## In short

A Section 13(2) notice should clearly state what is owed and which assets are at stake. Read it carefully, compare it with your loan records, reply in writing within the notice period, and seek advice early, because the time limits are short.
