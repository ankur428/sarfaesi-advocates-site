When a bank enforces its security under the SARFAESI Act 2002, the person occupying the property is not always the borrower. Sometimes it is a lessee, who pays rent under a lease and has no loan with the bank. The Supreme Court's decision in *Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. International Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd* (2014) 6 SCC 1 is commonly cited on the rights of such lessees.

## Why lessees are a special case

A lessee did not take the loan and did not default. Yet a possession action aimed at the borrower's property can affect the lessee's occupation. The case is cited for the idea that a lessee's rights under a lease are not automatically wiped out merely because the owner's property is secured to a lender.

## The core idea in plain terms

The decision recognises that a lessee may have rights of its own in the property. Whether those rights are protected depends on the facts, and on how and when the lease came into existence. Two broad questions usually matter:

- Was the lease genuine, or only a paper arrangement made to resist the bank?
- Was it created in a way the law permits, taking into account the mortgage and any terms the lender had agreed to?

The exact tests and their limits should be read from the judgment itself and checked against the current text of the Act.

## What a lessee should do

If you are a lessee and receive a possession notice or find that a bank is proceeding against the property:

- Collect the lease deed, rent receipts, proof of registration and any stamp duty paid. These show what your rights are and when they began.
- Note the dates carefully. The date of the lease compared with the date of the mortgage can matter.
- Keep every notice and communication from the bank, as it shows which step has been taken.
- Act quickly. A person aggrieved by a SARFAESI measure can generally apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, and a time limit applies. Check the current text of the Act for the period.

## What this means for banks and buyers

Lenders and auction purchasers also need to look at who is in occupation and on what basis. A lease on record is a fact to be examined, not ignored, and it can affect what physical possession will practically involve.

## A word of caution

*Harshad Govardhan Sondagar* decided specific questions on the rights of lessees, and it should not be read as protecting every occupant in every situation. A tenancy that is not genuine, or that does not meet the legal requirements, may be treated differently. Each case turns on its own documents and dates. This article is general information, not advice on any particular dispute.
