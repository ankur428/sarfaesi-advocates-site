Once a bank issues a notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act 2002, many borrowers think of selling or leasing the property to raise money quickly. Section 13(13) places a restriction on doing so without the lender's involvement.

## What Section 13(13) provides

In plain terms, after receiving a Section 13(2) notice, a borrower may not transfer the secured asset referred to in the notice, whether by sale, lease or otherwise (other than in the ordinary course of business), without the prior written consent of the secured creditor. Check the current text of the Act for the exact wording and its exceptions.

## Why the provision exists

The secured asset is the bank's security for the loan. If the borrower could sell or lease it freely once recovery action has begun, the security could be diminished or placed beyond the bank's reach. The provision protects the bank's position while the notice process is running.

## Practical points for borrowers

- **Get written consent first.** A verbal assurance from a bank officer is not a safe basis for a sale or lease. If you want to sell to clear the dues, ask the bank in writing and keep a copy.
- **Consider a sale with the bank's cooperation.** Many borrowers repay the loan from the sale proceeds. This works best when the bank agrees to the buyer, the price and the way the proceeds are applied.
- **Be careful with leases and part-transfers.** A lease or a transfer of part of the asset can also fall within the restriction, so do not assume that something short of a full sale is permitted.
- **Keep the notice period in mind.** The notice gives the borrower a fixed period to discharge liabilities. Check the current text of the Act for the period and for what happens if the borrower does not pay.

## Buyers and tenants should also take care

Anyone planning to buy or take a lease of a property should ask whether a SARFAESI notice has been issued against it. Searching the Central Registry, checking the title documents and asking the seller for the loan papers can show whether the property is already secured to a lender. For the position of lessees, see our article on *Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. IARC* (2014) 6 SCC 1.

## Remedies if you disagree with the bank

A borrower who feels aggrieved by a measure taken by the bank under Section 13(4) can approach the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, within the time limit in the Act. Under *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311, a borrower may also raise objections to the notice, and the bank must reply with reasons.

## In short

After a Section 13(2) notice, treat the secured property as off-limits for sale or lease unless the bank agrees in writing. If you are considering a transfer, take advice on your own facts before signing anything. This article is general information, not advice on any particular matter.
