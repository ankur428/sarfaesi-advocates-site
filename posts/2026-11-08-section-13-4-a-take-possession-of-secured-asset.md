Once the notice period under Section 13(2) ends without payment, Section 13(4) lists the measures a secured creditor may take. The first of these, Section 13(4)(a), is the power to take possession of the secured asset, including the right to transfer it by way of lease, assignment or sale for realising the asset. The Act has been amended over the years, so always check the current text.

## What the power covers

Taking possession is usually the step that makes the enforcement real for the borrower. It is not a standalone act: it follows a valid demand notice, a default that has been properly classified, and compliance with the procedure in the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002.

## Procedure that must be followed

The Rules, in particular Rule 8, set out how possession is taken and notified. The Supreme Court in *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610 stressed that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with. Possession and sale that ignore these Rules can be open to challenge.

## Symbolic and physical possession

In practice, a bank may first take symbolic possession by issuing a possession notice and publishing it, and later seek physical possession. Where the borrower or an occupant does not hand over the asset voluntarily, the bank may need to seek assistance under Section 14 of the Act. Check the current text of the Act for the exact procedure.

## Points a borrower can check

If you receive a possession notice, consider checking whether:

- The Section 13(2) notice was properly issued and the notice period had expired.
- The possession notice describes the correct asset and the amount due.
- The notice was published and served as the Rules require.
- Your reply to the earlier notice, if any, was answered with reasons, as *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311 contemplates.
- The asset taken is the one actually covered by the security.

## Challenging the action

A person aggrieved by measures taken under Section 13(4) may consider a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, within the time stated in the Act. In *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged writ petitions where an effective statutory remedy is available. Lessees and occupants with their own rights should also read *Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. IARC* (2014) 6 SCC 1.

## In short

Section 13(4)(a) gives a bank the power to take possession of the secured asset and to transfer it for realisation, but only after the notice stage and in line with the Rules. Keep every notice, note the dates, and take advice early, because the time limits for challenging the action are short.
