Auction default forfeiture is what follows when the successful bidder in a SARFAESI sale does not pay the price within the time allowed. Under Rule 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002, the deposit already paid can be forfeited and the property can be sold again. This article explains how that works for purchasers, borrowers and bidders.

## What is auction default forfeiture under Rule 9?

Rule 9 sets out the payment steps after a bid is accepted. The purchaser pays a part of the price at once and the balance within a fixed period, which can be extended only as the Rules allow and usually by written agreement. Check the current text of Rule 9 for the percentage and the number of days.

If the purchaser does not pay the balance within the time allowed, the Rules provide that the amount deposited may be forfeited to the secured creditor and the property may be put up for sale again. The defaulting purchaser also loses any claim to the property or to the amount forfeited. The overall sequence is explained in our [SARFAESI auction timeline](/blog/2026-11-28-sarfaesi-auction-timeline).

## What does the purchaser lose?

A purchaser who defaults can expect the following:

- The deposit paid on the fall of the hammer may be forfeited.
- No sale certificate is issued, because the price has not been paid in full.
- The right to the property ends, and the bank may sell it again.

The earnest money paid with the bid is a separate point. Our post on [auction EMD refunds](/blog/2026-11-30-auction-earnest-money-refund) explains when that money comes back and when it does not.

## What happens to the property after a default?

The secured creditor may hold a fresh sale. A re-sale is a new sale, so the notice and publication requirements apply again. See our note on [what happens when a bank auction fails](/blog/2026-12-02-auction-fails-reauction) for the steps that follow. The bank may also use another mode of sale where Rule 8 permits it.

## What does this mean for the borrower?

A purchaser's default does not clear the loan. Amounts realised from a later sale go first to the dues and costs, and the borrower remains liable for any balance, subject to the Act. Any forfeited money is treated under the Rules, so ask the authorised officer in writing how it was applied and keep the reply.

A borrower can also look at the sale itself. Where the Rules were not followed, the borrower may challenge the action. In Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610, the Supreme Court stressed that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with. Our page on [SARFAESI auction disputes](/sarfaesi-auction-disputes) sets out the common grounds.

## How can a bidder avoid forfeiture?

1. Read the sale notice for the payment dates before you bid.
2. Arrange funds, including any loan, before the auction date.
3. If you need more time, ask the bank for an extension in writing before the deadline, and act only on a written reply.
4. Check the title and encumbrances before bidding, since doubts found later do not automatically excuse a default.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the bank forfeit my deposit if I could not arrange the balance?
The Rules allow forfeiture where the balance is not paid within the permitted time. Check the current text of Rule 9 for the periods and whether any extension applies.

### Can a defaulting purchaser challenge the forfeiture?
A purchaser who believes the bank acted contrary to the Rules or the sale notice may seek legal advice on the available remedy. The outcome depends on the facts and the terms of the notice.

### Does a purchaser's default end the bank's right to sell?
No. The security remains with the bank, which may sell the property again in the manner the Rules allow.
