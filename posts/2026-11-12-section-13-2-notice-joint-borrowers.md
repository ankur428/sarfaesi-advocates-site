A SARFAESI notice to joint borrowers should be addressed to the borrower under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act 2002, and in practice every co-applicant who shares the loan should be named and served. The Act's definition of "borrower" is wide enough to cover persons who stand behind the loan, such as guarantors and those who have mortgaged property as security. If you are a co-applicant who has not received anything, it is worth finding out why.

## Who counts as a borrower under the Act?

The Act does not limit the word to the first name on the loan form. A person who has given a guarantee, or who has created a mortgage or pledge to secure the financial assistance, can also fall within the definition. Check the current text of the Act for the exact wording.

This matters because the notice is the first step before the secured creditor can take any measure under Section 13(4). Our guide on [what a SARFAESI notice must contain](/blog/2026-11-07-section-13-3-what-a-sarfaesi-notice-must-contain) sets out what the bank has to state, and the same discipline applies to naming the persons who are liable.

## Does every co-applicant need a separate notice?

The safer and usual practice is a notice to each person the bank treats as liable, at that person's own address. A single envelope addressed to the first applicant may not reach the others, and a person who never learns of the demand loses the chance to reply or arrange payment.

Whether a lapse in service makes later action unsafe depends on the facts, such as whether the person actually knew of the notice and whether anyone was prejudiced. Do not assume a gap in service will help you, and do not stay silent for that reason.

## What should each joint borrower do on receiving the notice?

Each person who receives the notice has their own sixty days. Sensible steps are:

1. Note the date and mode of receipt, and keep the envelope or delivery proof.
2. Compare the amount demanded with the loan account and your own payment records.
3. Check how the notice describes the secured asset and whether it names all those bound by the loan.
4. Send a written reply, either jointly or separately, asking the bank to respond with reasons.

*Mardia Chemicals v. UOI* (2004) 4 SCC 311 recognised that a borrower can object to the notice and that the creditor must reply with reasons. Co-applicants can each use that right, and a coordinated reply avoids contradictory statements about the same loan.

## When your interests differ from the other applicant

Co-borrowers do not always agree. One may have used the money, one may have left the business, and one may be a family member who signed as a co-applicant only. Your liability turns on the loan documents you signed, not on who benefitted. Keep copies of the sanction letter, agreement and any security documents. Where a person is only a guarantor, see our article on [guarantors and their defences](/blog/2026-10-04-guarantors-sarfaesi-liability-defences).

A reply sent by one co-applicant may not protect the others, so each person should consider a reply in their own name. Our page on the [Section 13(2) notice reply](/section-13-2-notice-reply) explains how a written reply fits into the process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can the bank send the notice to only one of the joint borrowers?
The notice should go to those the bank treats as liable under the loan. If you were left out, raise the point in writing and check the current text of the Act and the Rules on service.

### If I was not served, is the notice void against me?
Not automatically. The effect depends on the facts, including whether you knew of it and whether you were prejudiced, so seek advice on your own papers instead of assuming.

### Does my co-applicant's reply cover me too?
Not safely. Each person can reply in their own name, and doing so keeps your objections on record if the bank proceeds under Section 13(4).
