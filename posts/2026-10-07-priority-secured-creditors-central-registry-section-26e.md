When a borrower owes money to several parties, it matters who gets paid first. The SARFAESI Act 2002 contains provisions on **registration of security interests** with the Central Registry (commonly called CERSAI) and on the **priority of secured creditors** in **Section 26E**.

## What is the Central Registry?

The Central Registry is a database where transactions such as securitisation, reconstruction of financial assets and the creation of security interests are recorded. The purpose is to give public notice of existing charges, so that a lender or buyer can check whether an asset is already encumbered.

## What does Section 26E provide?

In broad terms, Section 26E says that after a security interest has been registered with the Central Registry, the debts due to the secured creditor are to be paid in **priority over other debts and over government dues**, subject to certain exceptions. The exact wording and exceptions, including how it works with other laws, should be read from the **current text of the Act**.

## Why registration matters to lenders and buyers

- A lender who registers its charge has a clear record of its security.
- A buyer or another lender can search the registry before dealing with the property.
- Where two secured creditors claim the same asset, the order of registration can matter.

## Why it matters to borrowers

Priority becomes relevant when the borrower has multiple lenders, tax dues or other claims. A borrower or guarantor can ask:

1. Has the bank's security been registered, and when?
2. Are there other charges recorded against the same property?
3. Does the bank's claim in the notice match the registered charge?

## Auction buyers and priority

Anyone planning to buy at a bank auction should check the Central Registry along with the property title, because a prior charge in favour of another lender could affect the property. The due-diligence checklist for auction buyers on this blog covers other points to examine.

## Related remedies

If a borrower believes that a bank has acted contrary to the Act, a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal is the usual remedy, within the time limit in the Act. The Supreme Court in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110 discouraged writ petitions where this remedy exists.

## Getting advice

Priority questions depend on registration dates, security documents and the rules that apply to each claim. A careful document review is the best starting point.
