Yes, a bank can enforce a property you mortgaged for someone else's loan if the loan becomes a non-performing asset and the mortgage is valid. In a third party mortgage SARFAESI case, the property itself is the security, so the bank can proceed against it even though you did not take the loan. What the bank can recover from you beyond that property depends on the documents you signed.

## What is a third party mortgage?

A third party mortgage arises when an owner mortgages their own property to secure a loan taken by another person, such as a relative, a friend or a company. The owner is the mortgagor, and the person who took the loan is the principal borrower.

The mortgage deed creates a security interest in the property. That security interest is what the secured creditor enforces under Section 13(4) of the SARFAESI Act, after the notice and notice period under Section 13(2).

## Does the bank have to send me a notice?

The Act's definition of "borrower" includes a person who has given security or a guarantee for financial assistance granted to someone else. A mortgagor of a third party loan should therefore expect to be treated as a person entitled to the Section 13(2) notice, and the notice should describe the secured asset.

If you were not served, or the notice does not describe your property correctly, record that and keep a copy of whatever you did receive. Our post on [joint borrowers and who gets the notice](/blog/2026-11-12-section-13-2-notice-joint-borrowers) explains how notices to several persons are usually handled.

## How far does your liability go?

This depends on the papers. Two situations are common:

- If you only mortgaged the property and signed no guarantee, the bank's claim against you generally rests on the mortgaged property.
- If you also signed a guarantee, your liability may be co-extensive with that of the principal borrower under Section 128 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, unless the guarantee says otherwise.

Read the mortgage deed, any guarantee, the sanction letter and the loan agreement together before deciding on a response. Our page on [guarantors and mortgagors under SARFAESI](/guarantors-sarfaesi-enforcement) covers the general position.

## What can a third party mortgagor do?

Steps usually considered, in this order:

1. Collect the mortgage deed, the notice and the loan account statement.
2. Send a written objection under Section 13(3A) within the notice period. If the bank does not accept it, it must give reasons (Mardia Chemicals v. UOI (2004) 4 SCC 311).
3. Check whether the bank followed the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules before any possession or sale (Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610).
4. If aggrieved by a Section 13(4) measure, consider an application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, within the time the Act provides. Check the current text of the Act for the period.

You may also have a right to redeem the property by paying the dues before the sale is completed. Our post on [the right of redemption under Section 13(8)](/blog/2026-10-07-right-of-redemption-section-13-8) explains how that works.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a bank sell my property for someone else's loan?
It can if you mortgaged the property as security and the loan has become a non-performing asset, subject to following the Act and the Rules. The mortgage deed is the starting point.

### Am I personally liable if I only mortgaged my property?
That depends on the documents. If you signed only a mortgage, the claim is generally against the property, but a separate guarantee can make you liable for the debt.

### Can I challenge the sale of a mortgaged property before the DRT?
A person aggrieved by a Section 13(4) measure can apply under Section 17. The grounds depend on the facts, the notice and the bank's compliance with the Rules.
