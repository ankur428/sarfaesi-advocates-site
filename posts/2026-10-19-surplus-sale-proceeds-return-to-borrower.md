Sometimes a secured property sells for more than the amount the borrower owes. A natural question follows: **does the borrower get the extra money back?** The SARFAESI Act 2002 deals with this in its provisions on how sale money is to be applied.

## What the Act says about sale proceeds

Under **Section 13(7)**, money received by a secured creditor from a sale is to be held **in trust** and applied in a stated order: first to the costs of the sale and incidental expenses, then to the dues of the secured creditor. The **residue**, if any, is to be paid to the person entitled to it, subject to the Act. **Check the current text of Section 13(7)** and the Rules for the exact wording.

## What this means in plain terms

1. The bank may deduct the **reasonable costs** of the sale.
2. It then adjusts the **secured debt**, including interest and charges that are properly due.
3. If anything remains, it should be paid to the borrower or to another person who has a legal right to it, such as a subsequent secured creditor.

## When there may be competing claims

A surplus may not always go to the borrower. If another lender or claimant has a charge on the property, that claim may need to be considered first. This is why priority and registration of charges matter, as explained in our post on the Central Registry.

## What a borrower should do

- **Ask for a sale statement** showing the sale price, costs deducted and the amount applied to the loan.
- **Compare it with the account statement** before the sale.
- **Write to the bank** asking for the surplus, if any, to be paid, with account details.
- **Keep copies** of the sale notice, auction result and sale certificate.
- If the bank does not respond, take advice on the proper forum and timeline.

## Also consider the shortfall

If the sale price is **lower** than the dues, the bank may claim the balance through other recovery routes. The Supreme Court in *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) 1 SCC 125 held that SARFAESI and the RDB Act operate together, so a shortfall may be pursued that way.

## Challenging the sale itself

If the borrower believes the sale was defective, the remedy is a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal within the time in the Act. The Supreme Court in *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610 stressed compliance with Rules 8 and 9.

## Getting advice

Whether there is a surplus, and who is entitled to it, depends on the sale papers and competing claims. A prompt review helps in making a clear written demand.
