A bank auction property title check means verifying, before you bid, that the borrower or mortgagor actually owned the property and that no other claim stands against it. Because most SARFAESI sales are on an as is where is basis, the bidder carries much of this risk. This article explains the checks a careful bidder, and anyone affected by the sale, should understand.

## Why does a title check matter in a SARFAESI auction?

A secured creditor can sell only the interest it holds in the property. If the borrower's title was defective, or if another person has a prior or competing right, the bank cannot pass on more than it has. The sale notice describes the property, but it does not replace your own verification.

Our post on [as is where is bank auctions](/blog/2026-12-04-auction-as-is-where-is) explains why condition and title risks fall on the bidder. A title check deals with the second of those risks.

## What documents should you examine?

Start with the sale notice and then ask for, or inspect, the papers the notice mentions. Typical items include:

- The chain of title deeds showing how the borrower or mortgagor acquired the property.
- The mortgage deed and any record of the mortgage with the registering authority.
- Approved plans, completion or occupancy papers, and land records, where they apply to the property.
- Property tax receipts and records of dues to a society, if any.

If a paper is not available for inspection, ask the authorised officer in writing and keep the reply.

## What encumbrances should you look for?

An encumbrance is any charge or claim on the property. Search the relevant registration records and land records for the period that your advisor considers sensible. Look in particular for:

- Other mortgages or charges, including those of other lenders.
- Attachments, court orders or pending litigation that mention the property.
- Claims by co-owners, family members or others with an interest.
- Leases or tenancies, which are covered further below.

Check the current text of Rule 8 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 for what the sale notice must state about encumbrances known to the secured creditor. Remember that "known to the secured creditor" is not the same as "all that exist".

## What about tenants and occupants?

A property may be occupied by a tenant or lessee. The rights of lessees were considered in Harshad Govardhan Sondagar v. IARC (2014) 6 SCC 1, and their position depends on the lease and the law. Inspect the property on the dates the notice allows and ask who occupies it. Our page for [tenants and occupants](/tenants-occupants-sarfaesi-possession) explains how occupants are treated.

## How does a title check fit with the other pre-bid steps?

Use the title check together with the other steps in our [buying at a SARFAESI auction checklist](/blog/2026-10-06-buying-at-sarfaesi-auction-checklist): read the notice, check the reserve price and dates, inspect the property and confirm how and when the price must be paid. If a title defect later emerges, the options depend on the terms of sale and the Rules, and disputes are explained on our page on [SARFAESI auction disputes](/sarfaesi-auction-disputes).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the bank guarantee the title of an auctioned property?
Not as a general rule. The bank sells the interest it holds as secured creditor, usually on an as is where is basis. Read the terms of the notice and verify the title yourself.

### Who should carry out a title check?
Ask a qualified advocate to examine the papers and records. This article gives general information and is not advice on a particular property.

### Can I check title after I have already paid the earnest money?
Practically it is late. Refund of earnest money depends on the notice and the Rules, so complete your checks before you bid.
