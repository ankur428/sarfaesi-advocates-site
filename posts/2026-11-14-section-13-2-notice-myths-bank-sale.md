Among the most common SARFAESI myths is the belief that a bank can sell your property overnight without telling you. Under the SARFAESI Act 2002 and the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002, a secured creditor must follow a sequence of written steps before a sale, and a sale that skips them can be challenged.

## Myth: the bank can sell without any notice

A secured creditor must first issue a demand notice under Section 13(2) and allow the borrower time to discharge the liability in full. Only after that period ends can it take measures under Section 13(4), such as taking possession of the secured asset.

Even then, a sale is a separate step. The Rules require a possession notice and a notice of sale, and the Supreme Court in *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610 stressed that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with. Check the current text of the Rules for the exact notice periods.

## Myth: you cannot object to the notice

Many borrowers think a SARFAESI notice is final. In *Mardia Chemicals v. UOI* (2004) 4 SCC 311, the Supreme Court upheld the Act but recognised that a borrower can object to the notice and that the creditor must reply with reasons.

A written objection is therefore part of the process, not a formality. Our guide to [mistakes borrowers make after a SARFAESI notice](/blog/2026-11-13-section-13-2-notice-common-mistakes) explains why a prompt, specific reply matters.

## Myth: the bank needs a court order to act

In general, a secured creditor acts under the Act itself and does not need to start a suit first. A court or tribunal order is needed in particular situations, for example when the bank asks the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or District Magistrate for help with possession under Section 14.

This does not leave the borrower without a remedy. A person aggrieved by a measure under Section 13(4) can file an application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17. See our page on [Section 17 applications](/section-17-drt-application) for how that route works.

## Myth: a civil suit or a writ is always the best answer

Civil courts are barred from entertaining matters the DRT can decide, under Section 34. High Courts have also been discouraged from using writ jurisdiction where an effective remedy exists, as seen in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110.

## Myth: talking to the bank stops the process

Discussions with a branch or a recovery agent do not pause the statutory steps unless the bank confirms a pause in writing. Keep records of every date and every document you receive. Our page on the [Section 13(2) notice reply](/section-13-2-notice-reply) covers what to put in writing.

## Points worth remembering

- The notice period runs from receipt, so record when and how the notice reached you.
- A reply, a Section 17 application and a settlement proposal can be pursued as the facts allow.
- Time limits for filing a Section 17 application should be checked against the current text of the Act.
- Documents such as the loan statement, sanction letter and sale notice are worth keeping together.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a bank sell my property without telling me?
Not lawfully if it skips the required notices. The Act and Rules set out a demand notice, a possession notice and a sale notice, and a sale that ignores them can be questioned before the DRT.

### Does a bank need a court order to take possession under SARFAESI?
Not always. It may take possession under Section 13(4)(a) on its own, though it may approach a magistrate under Section 14 where it needs help getting possession.

### Is the borrower's objection to a SARFAESI notice binding on the bank?
The bank is not bound to accept it, but after *Mardia Chemicals* it must consider the objection and give a reasoned reply to the borrower.
