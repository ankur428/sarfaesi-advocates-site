The grounds to challenge SARFAESI action are, in essence, that a measure taken by the bank under Section 13(4) was not in accordance with the SARFAESI Act 2002 or the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002. A borrower, guarantor or other affected person raises these grounds in a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT).

## What Section 17 allows you to challenge

Section 17 lets a person aggrieved by any measure taken by the secured creditor under Section 13(4) approach the DRT. The test is whether the measure complied with the Act and the Rules. The Tribunal does not simply ask whether money is owed; it looks at how the bank went about enforcing its security.

Because the application is tied to a particular measure, a ground only helps if it connects to the step you are challenging. A defect in the demand notice is a different ground from a defect in the sale. Our article on the [Section 17 application and its time limit](/blog/2026-10-02-section-17-application-drt) explains the basic framework.

## Grounds based on the demand notice

The Section 13(2) notice is the foundation of everything that follows. Common grounds include:

- The account was not properly classified as a non-performing asset before the notice was issued.
- The notice does not state the amount due and the secured assets, as Section 13(3) requires.
- The notice was not served on all the persons who should have received it, such as joint borrowers or guarantors.
- The amount claimed is wrong because of errors in the loan account.

If you objected to the notice, the bank is expected to give reasons for rejecting your objections. *Mardia Chemicals v. UOI* (2004) 4 SCC 311 recognises that a borrower can object and the creditor must reply with reasons. A bank that ignored or brushed aside your reply may have acted contrary to that principle. See our note on [objecting to a Section 13(2) notice](/blog/2026-10-21-objecting-to-section-13-2-notice-bank-must-reply-reasons).

## Grounds based on possession and sale

Where the bank has taken possession or put the asset up for sale, the grounds usually turn on Rules 8 and 9. These rules govern the possession notice, the valuation and reserve price, the sale notice and the payment and confirmation steps. *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610 stresses that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with.

Typical questions are whether the required notice was given, whether the sale was properly advertised, whether the reserve price was fixed in the manner the Rules require, and whether the sale followed the stated timelines. Each depends on the documents, so keep the notices and sale papers together. Our [documents checklist for a Section 17 application](/blog/2026-11-16-section-17-application-documents-checklist) lists what to gather.

## Grounds that usually do not work

Some arguments rarely help on their own. Disputing that you took the loan at all, or arguing the bank should have chosen another remedy, does not by itself show non-compliance with the Act. Likewise, a minor procedural slip that caused no prejudice may carry little weight. It is better to put forward a few well-supported grounds than a long list of weak ones.

Remember also that the bank may pursue its recovery through more than one route. *Transcore v. UOI* (2008) 1 SCC 125 holds that SARFAESI and the RDB Act operate together.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I raise a new ground after filing?
This depends on the Tribunal's procedure and the stage of the case. It is safer to include every ground you rely on in the original application and check the current rules on amendment.

### Do I need proof for each ground?
Yes, each ground should be backed by documents such as notices, statements, receipts and dates of service. A ground without supporting papers is difficult to establish.

### Is a challenge possible if I owe the money?
A borrower can still ask whether the bank followed the law in enforcing. Owing a debt does not remove the requirement that each step comply with the Act and the Rules.
