When a bank takes action under the SARFAESI Act, a borrower often asks whether to file a writ petition in the High Court or an application under Section 17 before the Debts Recovery Tribunal. The writ vs Section 17 question matters because the Act itself gives a Section 17 remedy, and High Courts usually expect it to be used first. This article compares the two routes in plain terms.

## What is a Section 17 application?

Section 17 lets a borrower or any other person aggrieved by a measure taken under Section 13(4) apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal. The Tribunal can examine whether the measures taken by the secured creditor comply with the Act and the Rules. An appeal from its order lies to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 18. The procedure is explained on our [Section 17 page](/section-17-drt-application) and in the article on the [Section 17 step-by-step procedure](/blog/2026-11-15-section-17-application-step-by-step).

## What is a writ petition under Article 226?

A writ petition is filed in the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution. It is a discretionary, constitutional remedy. The court does not sit as a fact-finding forum in the way a Tribunal does, and it may refuse to hear a petition where another effective remedy exists. The general approach is covered in our article on [when a High Court hears a writ](/blog/2026-12-14-writ-when-high-court-hears).

## How do the two routes differ?

- **Source of the remedy:** Section 17 is a remedy created by the Act. A writ is a constitutional remedy that the court grants at its discretion.
- **Type of dispute:** Disputes about accounts, valuation, notice contents or sale procedure involve facts and documents, which suit the Tribunal. A pure question of law or an alleged lack of authority is what writ petitioners usually rely on.
- **Next step:** A Tribunal order can be appealed under Section 18. A High Court decision has its own appeal route.
- **Timing:** Section 17 has a limitation period set by the Act. Check the current text of the Act and count from the measure challenged.

## What did the Supreme Court say about the choice?

In United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged High Courts from entertaining writ petitions against SARFAESI action where the statutory remedy is available. Earlier, Mardia Chemicals v. UOI (2004) 4 SCC 311 had considered the validity of the Act and recognised the borrower's right to object and to receive reasons. The practical effect is that a petitioner who skips Section 17 should be ready to explain why. More is on the [High Court writ and SARFAESI](/high-court-writ-sarfaesi) page.

## How should you decide?

Questions worth asking before choosing:

1. Has a Section 13(4) measure been taken, so that Section 17 is open?
2. Is the dispute mainly about facts, figures or evidence?
3. Is there a real jurisdictional or legal point that needs no fact-finding?
4. Is the limitation period for Section 17 still running?

Because the Tribunal route has a time limit, waiting for a writ outcome can put the Section 17 remedy at risk. Anyone unsure should get the papers reviewed promptly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file both a writ petition and a Section 17 application?

Filing on the same dispute in two forums can raise objections, and the court or Tribunal may ask about the other proceeding. Disclose any parallel case fully and clearly.

### Which is faster, a writ or Section 17?

There is no fixed answer, since speed depends on the forum's workload and the facts. Do not choose a forum on that basis alone.

### What if the High Court sends me to the Tribunal?

You can then apply under Section 17, but the limitation period for that remedy should be checked at once. Check the current text of the Act on limitation and condonation.
