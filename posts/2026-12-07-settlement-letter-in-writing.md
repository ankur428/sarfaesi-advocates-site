A one time settlement letter is the bank's written record of the terms on which it agrees to close your loan account. Until you hold that letter, there is no settlement you can safely rely on, and enforcement steps under the SARFAESI Act 2002 may continue. This article explains what the letter should say and what to check before you pay anything.

## Why does the settlement letter matter?

A bank's officers may discuss figures and timelines in meetings or calls. Those discussions are not an agreement. The bank's sanction, issued in writing by an authorised person, is the document that fixes what you must pay and what the bank will do in return.

Without it, disputes later turn on memory. With it, both sides can point to the same terms. Our post on [preparing to negotiate a one time settlement](/blog/2026-12-06-settlement-negotiation-preparation) covers what to do before the offer; this one covers what to secure after the bank agrees.

## What should a one time settlement letter contain?

Read the letter line by line and check that it states, clearly:

- The exact settlement amount, and how it is arrived at from the dues.
- The date by which payment is due, and whether payment is in one sum or instalments, with each date and amount.
- How payment must be made, and into which account.
- What happens to interest and charges after the settlement is sanctioned.
- What the bank will do on full payment, including closure of the account and release of the security.

If any of these is missing or vague, ask the bank to correct the letter before you pay. A gap you leave open is a gap that can be argued about later.

## What should the letter say about the pending SARFAESI action?

Enforcement may already be under way. The letter should say what the bank will do about the notice, possession or sale steps once you pay. If a Section 17 application is pending, the letter should address how that case will be closed. Read about [settlement while a Section 17 application is pending](/blog/2026-11-19-section-17-settlement-while-pending) so that the case does not stay open after the dues are cleared.

Also look for what happens if you miss a date. Many letters say the sanction lapses on default. Know that consequence before you accept the terms.

## What happens after you pay?

Keep the proof of every payment. On full payment you should expect written confirmation from the bank that the settlement amount has been received and that the account is closed. Release of the title documents and any charge created on the property should follow, and you should keep a record of when and how that is done.

For the wider legal framework on payment, redemption and closure, see our page on [one-time settlement and redemption](/one-time-settlement-redemption).

## What if the bank only gives an oral assurance?

Do not treat an oral assurance as a settlement. Ask for the terms to be issued in writing and keep your legal timelines protected in the meantime. A borrower who stops replying to notices or lets a limitation period pass while waiting for a letter may lose rights that cannot be restored.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an email from the bank enough as a settlement letter?
It may record the terms, but what matters is that it states the amount, dates and consequences clearly and comes from someone authorised to sanction the settlement. If in doubt, ask for a signed sanction letter.

### Can I pay before I receive the written sanction?
It is risky. A payment made without a written sanction may be treated as a part payment, with no promise of closure attached to it. Get the terms in writing first.

### Can the bank change the terms after issuing the letter?
Ask the bank to confirm in the letter that the terms are final once you meet the payment dates. If the bank wants to vary them, insist that the variation is also recorded in writing.
