Under Section 133 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a surety is discharged from liability for later transactions if the creditor and the principal debtor vary the terms of the contract without the surety's consent. This guarantor discharge variation rule is often raised when a bank restructures or reschedules a loan, but whether it helps in a given case depends on the guarantee deed and on what exactly was changed.

## What does Section 133 say?

The section is about a change in the terms of the contract between the bank and the borrower. If the terms are varied without the guarantor's consent, the guarantor is discharged as to transactions after the variation.

Two points are worth noting:

- The variation must be a change to the terms of the contract, not merely a favour or a delay in practice.
- The discharge operates for transactions after the variation, not for what was already due.

Read the section's current text and the guarantee deed together, because the deed may contain clauses on consent to changes.

## What counts as a variation of terms?

Examples a guarantor may point to, if they are in the papers, include:

- A change in the rate of interest or the repayment schedule.
- An increase in the loan amount or a new facility added to the same account.
- A change in the security held for the loan.

Whether any of these is a material change, and whether it was made without the guarantor's consent, are questions of fact. A guarantor who signed the restructuring papers, or who is a director who approved the change, may find it harder to say consent was missing.

## Does the guarantee deed matter?

Yes. Many guarantee deeds say that the guarantor agrees in advance to variations, extensions or indulgences given to the borrower. Whether such a clause is enough to defeat a Section 133 argument depends on its wording and on the facts. Do not assume either way; read the deed.

Under Section 128 of the Contract Act, a guarantor's liability is generally co-extensive with the borrower's unless the contract says otherwise, so the burden of showing a discharge is a real one. Keep the original sanction letter, every later amendment and any restructuring letter side by side.

## How does this arise in SARFAESI proceedings?

A guarantor who has given a mortgage or whose property is secured may receive a Section 13(2) notice. A borrower or guarantor can object to the notice, and if the bank rejects the objection it must give reasons (Mardia Chemicals v. UOI (2004) 4 SCC 311). A discharge argument can be raised in that reply and later in a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, if a Section 13(4) measure is taken. Check the current text of the Act for the period to apply.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a guarantor discharged every time the loan is restructured?
Not automatically. It depends on what changed, whether the guarantor consented, and what the guarantee deed provides.

### Can a guarantor who signed the restructuring letter claim discharge?
Signing the restructuring papers may be treated as consent to the change. The wording of the papers and the facts decide the point.

### Does discharge under Section 133 cancel the amount already due?
The section speaks of discharge as to transactions after the variation. Dues that arose before the change are a separate question to be checked against the papers.
