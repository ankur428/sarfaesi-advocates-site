Receiving a demand notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act 2002 does not leave a borrower without a voice. The Act allows the borrower to **raise objections in writing**, and the secured creditor is expected to **respond with reasons**.

## Where this comes from

In *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311, the Supreme Court upheld the validity of the Act but recognised that a borrower must be able to object to the notice and that the creditor must reply to those objections with reasons. Parliament later added this safeguard to the Act as **Section 13(3A)**. **Check the current text of Section 13(3A)** for the exact wording and the time allowed for the bank's reply.

## What an objection can cover

A borrower may question the notice on factual or legal grounds, such as:

- the **amount claimed** is different from the loan account
- the **date of NPA classification** appears incorrect
- interest or charges seem **wrongly applied**
- the notice does not **describe the secured asset** correctly
- the person served is **not the correct borrower or guarantor**

## How to write a useful objection

1. **Reply in writing** within the period stated in the notice, and keep proof of delivery.
2. **Be specific.** Point to the entry, date or figure you dispute instead of making general complaints.
3. **Attach documents**, such as statements, receipts and earlier correspondence.
4. **Ask for supporting records**, such as the account statement and the basis of the NPA date.
5. **Stay respectful and factual.** A clear letter is easier to rely on later.

## What the bank's reply means

If the bank considers the objection, it should communicate its reasons for accepting or not accepting it. A reply that gives no reasons, or ignores the points raised, may be useful to point out in later proceedings.

## Objection is not the end of the road

If the objections are not accepted and the bank takes a measure under Section 13(4), the borrower can consider a **Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal** within the time limit in the Act. The Supreme Court in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110 discouraged writ petitions against SARFAESI action where such a remedy is available.

## Do not ignore the notice

Not replying can weaken your position and may leave you with less on record. The objection should be sent even if you also plan to discuss a settlement with the bank.

## Getting advice

Every notice and loan account is different. A prompt review of the papers helps in preparing an objection that is accurate and complete.
