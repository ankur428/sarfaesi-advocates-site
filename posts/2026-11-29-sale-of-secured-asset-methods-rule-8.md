SARFAESI sale methods are the ways in which a secured creditor may sell an immovable secured asset once it has taken possession. The Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 allow the authorised officer to choose among obtaining quotations, inviting tenders, holding a public auction or selling by private treaty. Knowing which method is being used tells a borrower or bidder which rules and notices to check.

## What are the methods of sale under Rule 8?

Rule 8 lists the permitted modes of sale of immovable property. In plain terms they are:

1. Obtaining quotations from persons dealing in similar assets or otherwise interested in buying.
2. Inviting tenders from the public.
3. Holding a public auction.
4. Sale by private treaty.

The authorised officer picks the method, but the choice is not unlimited in practice. The sale must be fair, the price must be justified by the valuation and reserve price, and the process must be one that can be explained later if challenged. Check the current text of Rule 8 for the exact wording and sub-rules.

## How do auction and tender differ?

In a public auction, bidders compete openly, and these days most bank auctions are held on an online platform. In a tender, bidders submit sealed or electronic offers by a stated date, and the offers are opened and compared. Both are public, which is why they are the usual choice for residential and commercial property.

For either method the sale notice should describe the asset, state the reserve price and give the time, place and terms of payment. ## When is a quotation or private treaty used?

Quotations and private treaty involve fewer people than a public sale. The creditor approaches likely buyers directly, or negotiates with one buyer. Because there is less public competition, these routes need greater care over valuation and records, and they are more open to the objection that the asset was sold too cheaply or to a favoured buyer.

A borrower who learns that the bank is selling by one of these routes should ask for the valuation, the basis of the price and the notice given. Check the current text of Rules 8 and 9 for any conditions that apply to private treaty, including what must be disclosed to the borrower.

## What notice must the borrower receive?

Whatever the method, the Rules require notice of sale to the borrower, and the sale cannot take place before the minimum period from that notice has passed. The Supreme Court held in Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610 that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with, so a sale on a defective or short notice is open to challenge. ## Can a sale be challenged on the ground of the method used?

A borrower or other aggrieved person can apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17. Complaints about the method usually come down to the same points: no proper valuation, a reserve price not supported by the evidence, missing notice, or a process that was not open and fair. Our page on [SARFAESI auction disputes](/sarfaesi-auction-disputes) describes these grounds. The time limit for a Section 17 application is fixed by the Act, so check its current text and act promptly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a bank sell my property without a public auction?
The Rules allow other methods, such as quotations, tenders and private treaty, as well as public auction. Whichever method is used, notice of sale to the borrower and compliance with Rules 8 and 9 are still required.

### Is a tender sale the same as an e-auction?
No. In a tender, bids are submitted by a closing date and then opened and compared, while an auction involves competitive bidding. Both can be run online, and the sale notice should say which applies.

### Which sale method gives the borrower the most protection?
The Rules do not rank the methods, and protection comes from compliance with notice, valuation and reserve price requirements. Public methods are generally easier to show as open and fair.
