A writ petition against SARFAESI action is filed in the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution, but the High Court does not hear every such petition on the merits. Where the Act gives the borrower an efficient alternative remedy before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, the court will usually ask the petitioner to use it first. This article explains how a writ petition SARFAESI High Court challenge is treated and what a petitioner should be ready to show.

## Why are writ petitions discouraged in SARFAESI matters?

The Act provides a statutory remedy: an application to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, followed by an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 18. In United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged High Courts from entertaining writ petitions against SARFAESI action when this remedy is available.

The idea is simple. The Tribunal can examine facts, accounts and documents, which a High Court exercising writ jurisdiction ordinarily does not do. A fuller discussion is in our article on [Satyawati Tondon and writ petitions](/blog/2026-10-30-united-bank-of-india-v-satyawati-tondon-writ-petitions-sarfaesi).

## When might a High Court still hear a writ petition?

The availability of another remedy is a rule of practice and discretion in writ law, not a complete bar. Petitioners usually try to show a case of a kind courts have traditionally been willing to examine, such as:

- the action is said to be without jurisdiction or without authority of law;
- there is an alleged breach of natural justice or of a mandatory statutory step;
- a point of law that goes to the root of the action, with no real dispute on facts;
- a challenge to the validity of a provision, which the Supreme Court has already considered in Mardia Chemicals v. UOI (2004) 4 SCC 311.

Whether the court agrees is a matter of its discretion on the facts of each petition. No particular result can be assumed.

## What does the High Court look at first?

Before the merits, the court commonly asks:

1. Has a Section 13(4) measure actually been taken, so that a Section 17 application is open?
2. Is the dispute mainly about facts, such as the amount due or the valuation?
3. Has the petitioner come promptly and with complete papers?
4. Is there an adequate reason for not going to the Tribunal?

A petition that is really a dispute about accounts or sale price is likely to be sent to the Tribunal. For a comparison of the two routes, see the [High Court writ and SARFAESI](/high-court-writ-sarfaesi) page.

## What papers support a writ petition?

The petition should be self-contained and accurate. Typical material includes the loan documents, the Section 13(2) notice, the borrower's reply and the bank's response, any possession notice or sale notice, and a short dated list of events. The petitioner should state plainly why the Tribunal route is not suitable, rather than leaving the court to infer it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I file a writ petition instead of a Section 17 application?

You can file one, but the High Court may decline to hear it if an effective Section 17 remedy exists. The court decides this at its discretion, so the petition should explain why it should be heard.

### Is there a time limit for a Section 17 application?

Yes, the Act sets a period for a Section 17 application. Check the current text of the Act and count from the date of the measure being challenged.

### Does a writ petition stop the bank from acting?

Not automatically. Any interim protection is a matter for the court after it considers the petition.
