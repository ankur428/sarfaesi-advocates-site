A demand notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act 2002 is only the first step. If the borrower does not pay within the period given in the notice, the secured creditor may move to **Section 13(4)**, which lists the enforcement measures available to it.

## The measures in Section 13(4)

In broad terms, a secured creditor may:

- **take possession** of the secured assets, including the right to transfer them by lease, assignment or sale
- **take over the management** of the secured business, including the right to transfer it by lease, assignment or sale
- **appoint a manager** to manage the secured assets of which possession has been taken
- **require a person who owes money to the borrower** to pay the secured creditor instead

**Check the current text of Section 13(4)** for the exact wording, as the provision has been amended over the years.

## Conditions before the bank can act

The bank cannot jump to these measures at will. Points to look at in your papers:

1. A valid **Section 13(2) notice** must have been issued, and the borrower's account must have been classified as a non-performing asset.
2. The **notice period** stated in the notice must have run out.
3. The **borrower's objections**, if any, should have been answered with reasons, as discussed in *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311.
4. The notice must **identify the secured assets** the bank intends to enforce against.

## Procedure still matters

Taking possession is not just a letter. The Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 set out how possession notices are issued and published, and how a sale is carried out. In *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610, the Supreme Court stressed that Rules 8 and 9 must be complied with. A defect in that process can be a ground to question the action.

## Where a borrower can go

A borrower aggrieved by a measure under Section 13(4) can consider a **Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal**, within the limitation period in the Act. In *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged writ petitions against SARFAESI action where an effective remedy exists.

## Practical steps

- Read the notice and any later **possession notice** carefully, and note every date.
- Keep copies of your **objection and the bank's reply**.
- Compare the amount demanded with your **loan account statement**.
- Do not ignore the deadline for a Section 17 application.
- Consider whether a **settlement or repayment plan** is realistic, and record any agreement in writing.

## Getting advice

Each case depends on its own documents and dates. A timely review of the notices and the loan record helps in understanding which options are still open.
