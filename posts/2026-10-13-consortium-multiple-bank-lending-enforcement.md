Businesses frequently borrow from more than one bank, either under a **consortium** or a **multiple banking arrangement**. When such loans turn bad, enforcement under the SARFAESI Act 2002 becomes more complex than in a single-lender case.

## Consortium vs multiple banking

In a consortium, a group of banks lends under one arrangement, usually with a lead bank, shared security and common documents. In multiple banking, each bank lends separately, often with its own security. The rules on enforcement, and the security documents themselves, may differ in the two models.

## What the Act requires

The Act contains a specific provision, Section 13(9), for cases where more than one secured creditor has financed the borrower. It limits individual enforcement unless creditors representing a prescribed share in value agree. A separate post on this blog explains it. The details have been amended over the years, so **check the current text of the Act**.

## Practical issues in multi-lender cases

1. **Common security:** if one property secures all lenders, the question of who enforces and in what order becomes important.
2. **Separate security:** if each lender holds a different asset, each may issue its own notice.
3. **Different notices and dates:** a borrower may receive several notices, each with its own 60-day period.
4. **Inter-creditor arrangements:** agreements among the lenders may govern sharing of recoveries.
5. **Registration of charges:** the Central Registry can show which lender registered what.

## What a borrower should do

- Prepare a **master list of all loans**, lenders, sanction letters and security documents.
- Ask for a **statement of account** from each lender.
- **Reply to each notice** separately and in writing.
- Track every deadline, since the limit for a Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal runs from the relevant measure.
- Raise any objection about the lenders' agreement or consent in the reply and, where needed, before the Tribunal.

## Settlement and restructuring

Multi-lender cases may be easier to resolve through a coordinated proposal than through separate negotiations. Whether the lenders agree is a matter for them, and no outcome can be assumed.

## Related law

The Supreme Court in *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311 recognised the borrower's right to object and the creditor's duty to reply with reasons. In *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) 1 SCC 125, it held that SARFAESI and the RDB Act operate together, so lenders may use either or both routes.

## Getting advice

Multi-bank cases involve many documents and overlapping deadlines. Getting everything reviewed in one place helps in planning a clear response.
