A defective SARFAESI notice is a demand notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act 2002 that does not meet what the Act requires, or that contains errors about the debt or the security. Spotting such defects early lets you raise them in your reply and, if needed, before the Debts Recovery Tribunal. Not every flaw is serious, so the aim is to separate real defects from minor slips.

## Why defects in the notice matter

The notice is the first formal step before a secured creditor can take any measure under Section 13(4). The Supreme Court in *Mardia Chemicals v. UOI* (2004) 4 SCC 311 recognised that a borrower can object to the notice and that the creditor must reply with reasons. That right is easier to use when you can point to a specific defect rather than a general grievance.

Whether a defect affects the validity of later action depends on its nature and on the facts. Courts and tribunals look at substance, so treat the points below as things to check, not as guaranteed grounds.

## Defects in the content of the notice

Compare the notice with what Section 13(2) and 13(3) call for. Our article on [what a SARFAESI notice must contain](/blog/2026-11-07-section-13-3-what-a-sarfaesi-notice-must-contain) explains these requirements in more detail. Common problems include:

- no clear statement of the amount said to be due, or figures that do not add up
- the secured assets are not described, or are described wrongly
- the notice does not say that the account has been classified as a non-performing asset
- the period allowed for payment is missing or wrongly stated
- the notice does not warn of the consequences of not paying, including the measures under Section 13(4)

## Defects about the debt and the account

A notice can also be questioned because of what it says about the loan. Check whether payments you made are reflected, whether interest and charges match your loan documents, and whether the date of default is correct. If the figures differ from your records, say so with entries and dates. Our guide to [checking the loan account before replying](/blog/2026-10-20-checking-loan-account-before-replying-sarfaesi-notice) shows how to do this step by step.

## Defects in service and in who is named

Look at how and when the notice reached you. Note the date of receipt, the address used and the mode of delivery. Check whether every person the bank treats as liable, such as co-borrowers and guarantors, has been named and served as the Act and the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 contemplate. A notice sent to a wrong address, or not sent to a person bound by the loan, may be a ground to raise, though the effect depends on the facts.

## What to do if you find a defect

Do not ignore the notice because you think it is flawed. A defect does not remove the demand by itself, and the sixty-day period continues to run. A sensible approach is:

1. List each defect with the clause or figure it relates to.
2. Reply in writing within the notice period, setting out each point and asking the bank to respond with reasons.
3. Keep proof of delivery of your reply and of the notice you received.
4. If the bank rejects your objections and takes a measure, consider an application under Section 17 within the time the Act allows.

You can read more about [the Section 13(2) notice reply](/section-13-2-notice-reply) and how a written reply fits into the process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I ignore a defective SARFAESI notice?
It is unwise to do so. Even if you believe the notice is flawed, the notice period continues, so raise the defect in a written reply and keep your remedies open.

### Does one small error make the notice invalid?
Not necessarily. A minor clerical slip may not matter, while a missing or wrong statement about the amount or the secured asset may be more significant. The outcome depends on the facts.

### Where can I challenge a defective notice?
A borrower aggrieved by a measure taken after the notice can apply to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17. Check the current text of the Act for the time limit.
