A SARFAESI sale certificate is the document the authorised officer issues to the purchaser once the price has been paid in full for a secured asset sold under the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002. It records the sale and is the purchaser's main evidence of title. This article explains when it is issued, what it should contain and what a bidder or borrower should check.

## What is a SARFAESI sale certificate?

When a secured creditor sells immovable property under the Rules, the buyer does not receive a conventional sale deed signed by the borrower. Instead, the authorised officer of the secured creditor issues a certificate of sale. Rule 9 deals with the time of sale, the confirmation of the sale, the payment of the price and the issue of this certificate.

For the buyer, the certificate is the document that stands in place of a deed of transfer. For the borrower, its issue marks the point after which the sale is far harder to undo.

## When is the certificate issued?

The Rules link the certificate to payment. In general terms, the stages are:

1. The purchaser is declared the highest bidder and pays the deposit.
2. The secured creditor confirms the sale, subject to the Rules.
3. The purchaser pays the balance within the period the Rules and the sale notice allow.
4. On full payment, the authorised officer issues the sale certificate.

If the purchaser does not pay in time, the deposit may be forfeited and the property sold again. Check the current text of Rule 9 for the exact periods. The overall sequence is set out in our [SARFAESI auction timeline](/blog/2026-11-28-sarfaesi-auction-timeline).

## What should the certificate contain?

A certificate should clearly identify the property, the purchaser, the secured creditor, the borrower and the price paid. It should also state that the sale was made under the SARFAESI Act and the Rules. The rules prescribe a form for it, so check the current text of the Rules rather than relying on a copy from another matter.

A purchaser should check the following before relying on the document:

- The property description matches the sale notice and the title papers.
- The name of the purchaser is correct and complete.
- The certificate is signed by the authorised officer and bears the date of issue.

## Stamp duty and registration

A sale certificate for immovable property usually has to be stamped and registered under the law of the State where the property lies, because registration is what lets the purchaser's name enter the land and municipal records. Our article on [stamp duty and registration of the sale certificate](/blog/2026-10-10-stamp-duty-registration-sale-certificate-sarfaesi) covers this in more detail. Rates and procedures are State specific, so check the current notification.

## What can a borrower do about a certificate already issued?

A borrower who believes the sale was defective can challenge it through an application under Section 17 before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, within the time fixed by the Act. Common grounds include a defective sale notice, non-compliance with Rules 8 and 9, or an unfair valuation. In Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610, the Supreme Court held that Rules 8 and 9 must be complied with.

Because the certificate is usually issued only after the price is paid, delay is risky for a borrower who wants to contest. Our page on [SARFAESI auction disputes](/sarfaesi-auction-disputes) explains the grounds in more detail.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a sale certificate the same as a sale deed?
It serves the same purpose, since it is the document that evidences the transfer, but it is issued by the authorised officer and not signed by the borrower. It usually needs to be stamped and registered under the State law.

### Can a sale certificate be cancelled?
A tribunal can set aside a sale if it finds that the Act or the Rules were not followed, but each case depends on its facts. A Section 17 application must be filed within the time the Act allows.

### Who gets the sale certificate if the purchaser defaults?
No certificate is issued to a purchaser who has not paid the full price. The Rules allow the deposit to be forfeited and the property to be sold again.
