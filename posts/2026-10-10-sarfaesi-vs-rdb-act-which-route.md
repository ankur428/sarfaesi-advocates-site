When a loan turns bad, a bank usually has two statutory routes: enforcing its security directly under the SARFAESI Act 2002, or filing an application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal (DRT) under the Recovery of Debts Act 1993 (the RDB Act). Borrowers often confuse the two, so it helps to see how they differ.

## The SARFAESI route

Under SARFAESI, a secured creditor can enforce its security interest **without going to court or tribunal first**. The usual steps are:

1. Classification of the account as a non-performing asset.
2. A notice to the borrower under Section 13(2), calling for repayment.
3. If the borrower does not pay, measures under Section 13(4), such as taking possession of the secured asset or selling it.
4. Compliance with the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 for possession notices, valuation and sale.

The route is available only to a **secured creditor** holding a security interest over the asset.

## The RDB Act route

Under the RDB Act, the bank files an application before the DRT to recover the debt. The Tribunal hears both sides, decides the amount due, and issues a recovery certificate. Recovery follows through the modes the Act provides. This route is a full adjudication, so it is slower but includes a hearing before any final order.

## Why banks often use both

The two laws are not rivals. The Supreme Court in *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) 1 SCC 125 held that SARFAESI and the RDB Act operate together, so a bank can use the remedies available under both statutes. Which it starts with depends on things such as:

- whether the debt is secured, and by what kind of asset;
- how quickly the bank wants to take control of the property;
- whether the facts are likely to be disputed.

Check the current text of both Acts for the conditions and any monetary thresholds.

## What this means for a borrower

- A SARFAESI notice is not a court order, but it starts strict timelines. **Read it carefully and respond in writing.**
- Objections to a SARFAESI measure are raised before the DRT through a Section 17 application. Courts have discouraged writ petitions where this remedy exists, as in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110.
- If the bank files a DRT application as well, both proceedings need to be tracked, and their dates diarised.
- Keep every notice, statement of account and proof of payment in one file.

## Getting advice

The right response depends on the loan documents, the security and the stage of the proceedings. Get the papers reviewed early, because limits on time for challenging bank action are short.
