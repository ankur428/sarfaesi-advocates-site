A Section 14 SARFAESI application is the bank's request to the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the District Magistrate for help in taking possession of a secured asset. The application is supported by an affidavit and documents that show the bank has followed the earlier steps of the Act. Check the current text of Section 14 before relying on any detail below.

## Who receives a Section 14 application?

The secured creditor applies to the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or the District Magistrate within whose jurisdiction the secured asset or other documents relating to it are found. The magistrate is asked to take possession of the asset and forward it to the secured creditor, or to help the creditor do so.

This stage is separate from the bank's own power to take possession under Section 13(4)(a). Our article on [taking possession under Section 13(4)(a)](/blog/2026-11-08-section-13-4-a-take-possession-of-secured-asset) explains that route, and the overview of [how courts assist banks under Section 14](/blog/2026-10-04-section-14-possession) shows where the magistrate fits in.

## What does the bank put before the magistrate?

The Act requires the application to be accompanied by an affidavit from the authorised officer of the secured creditor. The affidavit speaks to the steps the bank has already taken. Broadly, a careful application covers:

- the loan, the security and the fact that the account has been classified as a non-performing asset;
- the notice under Section 13(2) and the proof of how and when it was sent;
- any objection or representation made by the borrower and the bank's written reasons for rejecting it;
- the details of the secured asset, with its address and the documents that create the security; and
- the amount said to be due, with the statements of account.

The exact contents of the affidavit are set out in the Act, so the current text should be read directly. A borrower who receives notice of the application can compare the bank's statements against the papers in hand.

## What the magistrate looks at

The magistrate's role at this stage is limited. The magistrate checks that the application and affidavit meet the requirements of Section 14, and does not decide whether the debt is correct or whether the bank's action is valid. Those questions belong to the Debts Recovery Tribunal under Section 17, which is described on our page on [Section 17 applications before the DRT](/section-17-drt-application).

This is why a defect in the Section 13(2) notice or in the bank's reasons for rejecting an objection matters. It can be raised before the DRT, and a borrower should not wait for the possession order before thinking about that route.

## What can a borrower check when an application is filed?

Borrowers and occupants can ask a few simple questions:

1. Was a valid Section 13(2) notice sent, and did it reach the correct address?
2. Was the objection answered in writing with reasons?
3. Does the asset described in the application match the asset actually secured?
4. Is the asset within the magistrate's area?

Where something does not match, the discrepancy should be recorded with documents. No outcome should be assumed, because the result depends on the papers and the law.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the magistrate decide whether the bank's claim is correct?
No. At this stage the magistrate looks at whether the requirements of Section 14 are met. The correctness of the debt and the validity of the bank's steps are raised before the DRT under Section 17.

### Who signs the affidavit in a Section 14 application?
The Act requires an affidavit from the authorised officer of the secured creditor. Check the current text of Section 14 for the matters the affidavit must state.

### Can an occupant who is not the borrower be heard on the application?
Whether and how an occupant can be heard depends on the facts and the magistrate's procedure. An occupant should keep proof of the right to occupy, such as a lease, and seek advice early.
