When a secured creditor sells or otherwise transfers a secured asset after taking a measure under Section 13(4), what does the buyer actually receive? Section 13(6) of the SARFAESI Act addresses this. Check the current text of the Act for the exact wording.

## The basic idea

In general terms, Section 13(6) provides that a transfer of a secured asset by the secured creditor, in exercise of its rights under Section 13(4), vests in the transferee all rights in or in relation to that asset that the transferor had. The transfer is meant to work as if the borrower himself had made it.

For a buyer, this is the reason a bank sale can give a marketable title. For a borrower, it is a reminder that once a valid sale is complete, the asset is no longer his.

## What this does not mean

Section 13(6) does not make every sale immune from challenge. The provision speaks of a transfer made in exercise of the creditor's rights under the Act, so the way the creditor reached that point remains relevant. Points that are commonly examined include:

- Whether a valid Section 13(2) notice was given and the notice period respected.
- Whether the possession notice and sale notice complied with Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules, 2002. The Supreme Court stressed compliance with these Rules in *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610.
- Whether the borrower's objections were considered and answered with reasons, as recognised in *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311.

## Where disputes go

A person aggrieved by a measure under Section 13(4) may consider an application under Section 17 before the Debts Recovery Tribunal, subject to the time limit in the Act. In *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Supreme Court discouraged writ petitions where the Act offers an effective remedy, so the Tribunal is usually the natural first forum.

## Practical points for buyers

- Ask for the sale notice, the sale certificate and proof that the statutory notices were served.
- Check the property records for other encumbrances or pending proceedings.
- Do not assume that possession will be handed over automatically; the bank may need to seek assistance under Section 14 of the Act.

## Practical points for borrowers

- Raise objections to the process early, in writing, rather than after the sale is complete.
- Keep copies of every notice and proof of delivery.
- Take note of the limitation period for any application and do not leave it to the last days.

## In short

Section 13(6) is what gives a bank sale its legal effect, but that effect rests on a sale carried out in the manner the Act and Rules require. This article is general information, not advice on any particular matter.
