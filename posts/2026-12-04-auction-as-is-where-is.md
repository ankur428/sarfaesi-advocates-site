In an as is where is bank auction, the property is sold in the condition it is in and at the place where it stands, and the bank gives no assurance about its state. The phrase appears in most SARFAESI sale notices, so every bidder should know what it covers and what it does not. This article explains the wording in plain terms for bidders, borrowers and occupants.

## What does "as is where is" mean in a bank auction?

The phrase tells the bidder that the secured creditor is selling whatever interest it has, in the existing physical condition, at the existing location. The bank does not promise repairs, clearing of the site, or delivery in any better state than the notice describes.

Sale notices often add similar words such as "as is what is" or "without recourse". They are used for the same purpose, which is to place the risk of inspecting the property on the bidder. Read the exact wording of each notice, because the terms of sale are set out there.

## What risks does the bidder take?

A bidder who buys on this basis should expect to bear the following:

- The physical condition of the building or land, including defects not visible on a quick visit.
- The cost of taking over the property, which may include dealing with occupants.
- Dues that the notice itself says are not covered, such as electricity, water or society charges. Check the notice and the Rules to see what the notice must say.

For a practical list of checks before you bid, see our post on [buying at a SARFAESI auction](/blog/2026-10-06-buying-at-sarfaesi-auction-checklist). Title and encumbrance checks are a separate and equally important step.

## What does the phrase not excuse the bank from?

The wording does not allow a bank to skip the steps the law requires. Rules 8 and 9 of the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 govern the sale notice, the valuation and reserve price, the manner of sale and the payment of the price. Check the current text of Rule 8 for what the notice must contain, including the description of the property and the encumbrances known to the secured creditor.

In Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610, the Supreme Court stressed that Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with. A sale that ignores them is open to challenge by the borrower, whatever the notice says about condition. Our page on [SARFAESI auction disputes](/sarfaesi-auction-disputes) explains the usual grounds.

Nor does the phrase change what the bank actually owns to sell. The bank sells only the interest it holds as secured creditor, so a title problem is a different question from a condition problem.

## How should a bidder deal with an as is where is sale?

1. Read the whole sale notice, including the terms and conditions, before registering.
2. Inspect the property on the dates the notice gives for inspection, if it gives any.
3. Check the title papers and look for encumbrances. Our note on [e-auction bidding pitfalls](/blog/2026-10-18-e-auction-bidding-pitfalls-sarfaesi) lists common errors.
4. Ask the authorised officer in writing about anything unclear, and keep the reply.
5. Bid only a sum you can pay within the periods the Rules and the notice allow.

## What should a borrower or occupant note?

An as is where is clause concerns the buyer's risk. It does not end the borrower's right to object to the sale if the Rules were not followed, and it does not by itself decide the rights of tenants or other occupants. Those depend on their own papers and on the law.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I withdraw after bidding if I find a defect in the property?
Not as of right. Because the sale is on an as is where is basis, a defect that was open to inspection is generally the bidder's risk. Whether any money is refundable depends on the notice and the Rules, so read both.

### Does as is where is mean the bank hides defects in title?
No. The bank must still describe the property and the encumbrances known to it in the notice, as the Rules require. Check the current text of Rule 8 and verify the title yourself.

### Does the phrase stop a borrower from challenging the auction?
No. A borrower can still object if the notice, valuation or sale did not follow Rules 8 and 9, within the time the law allows.
