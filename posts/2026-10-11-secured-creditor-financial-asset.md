Before a lender can use the SARFAESI Act 2002, two threshold questions must be answered: is the lender a "secured creditor", and is the debt a "financial asset" backed by a "security interest"? If either answer is no, the SARFAESI route may not be open, which is why borrowers should look at these definitions early.

## Secured creditor

In plain terms, a secured creditor is a lender that holds a **security interest** in the borrower's asset. The Act's definition covers banks and financial institutions, and also other categories such as debenture trustees and asset reconstruction companies. The exact list is set out in the definitions section of the Act and has been amended over time, so **check the current text of the Act** before relying on it.

Two points are useful to remember:

- A lender without a security interest, such as one giving a purely unsecured loan, cannot enforce under Section 13.
- The security interest must be created in favour of the lender, usually by mortgage, hypothecation, pledge or charge under the loan documents.

## Financial asset

A financial asset broadly means a debt or receivable that is secured, such as a loan or advance given by the lender. The Act's definition lists the kinds of claims covered. Again, the precise wording is in the statute and should be read directly.

## Why these definitions matter to a borrower

A SARFAESI notice is only as strong as the basis on which it is issued. A borrower reviewing a notice can ask:

1. Is the sender a secured creditor within the Act?
2. Is there a valid security interest over the asset named in the notice?
3. Does the debt fall within the Act's definition of a financial asset?
4. Does the amount claimed match the loan documents and the statement of account?

If something looks wrong, the objection can be raised in reply to the notice and, where needed, before the Debts Recovery Tribunal in a Section 17 application. The Supreme Court in *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311 upheld the Act while recognising that a borrower can raise objections and that the creditor must reply with reasons.

## Practical steps

- Collect the sanction letter, loan agreement, and every security document in one file.
- Check who holds the security: the original lender, or an assignee such as an asset reconstruction company.
- Compare the property in the notice with the property actually mortgaged.
- Note the dates on every notice, because limits on time for challenging bank action are short.

## Getting advice

Whether a particular lender and debt fit the definitions depends on the documents and the current text of the Act. Having the papers reviewed early gives more room to respond.
