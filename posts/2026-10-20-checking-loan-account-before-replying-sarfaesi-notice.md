A demand notice under the SARFAESI Act 2002 states an amount the bank says is due. Before any reply is sent, the borrower should test that figure against the loan records. A reply that points to specific, documented discrepancies is far more useful than a general denial.

## Why the numbers matter

The notice is the foundation of everything that follows, including possession and sale. In *Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311, the Supreme Court recognised that a borrower can object to the notice and that the secured creditor must respond with reasons. An objection that identifies exact errors gives the bank something concrete to answer.

## Documents to gather

- The sanction letter and loan agreement, including the interest clause
- All security documents and any later amendment or restructuring letters
- Complete statements of account from the date of disbursal
- Receipts or bank entries for every payment made
- Earlier notices, emails and letters exchanged with the bank

## Points to check in the statement

1. **Payments credited.** Match each payment you made with an entry in the statement, and note the date it was credited.
2. **Interest rate.** Compare the rate applied over time with the rate in the agreement and any rate-change letters.
3. **Penal and other charges.** Check whether each charge has a basis in the loan documents.
4. **Dates.** Compare the date the account was classified as non-performing with the date mentioned in the notice.
5. **Amount in the notice.** See whether it breaks down principal, interest and charges, and whether the total matches the statement.

## Making the reply useful

- Keep the reply in writing and keep proof of delivery.
- Refer to specific entries and dates, not general complaints.
- Ask for any document you need but do not have, such as a full statement.
- Avoid admitting figures you have not verified.
- Keep a copy of everything sent and received.

## Remember the wider picture

Reviewing the account does not pause the timeline in the notice. Check the current text of the Act for the time limits that apply to the notice and to any response. If the reasons given by the bank are unsatisfactory, a borrower may consider a remedy before the Debts Recovery Tribunal. The Supreme Court in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110 discouraged writ petitions against SARFAESI action where an alternative remedy exists, so the record built at this stage can matter later.

Every case turns on its own documents, so a lawyer should review the papers before a reply is finalised.
