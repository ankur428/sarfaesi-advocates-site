When a bank enforces its security under the SARFAESI Act, the Security Interest (Enforcement) Rules 2002 lay down how possession is taken and how the property is sold. The Supreme Court's decision in *Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar* (2014) 5 SCC 610 is often cited for one clear message: **Rules 8 and 9 are to be complied with.**

## Why these Rules matter

Rule 8 deals with the steps a secured creditor follows when taking possession and preparing to sell secured property. Rule 9 deals with the sale itself, including notice to the borrower. The Rules exist so that a borrower knows what is happening to the property and has a fair chance to respond before it is sold.

Please check the current text of the Rules for the exact notice periods and procedure, as these can change by amendment.

## What the Supreme Court emphasised

In broad terms, the Court:

- treated compliance with Rules 8 and 9 as something the secured creditor must do, not an optional formality
- looked closely at whether the required steps were actually followed before the sale took place

The judgment does not mean every sale is invalid. It means the question of compliance is a real one that can be examined.

## What a borrower can look at

If a bank has issued a possession notice or a sale notice, it can help to go through the paperwork carefully:

1. **Collect every notice** the bank has sent, with envelopes, postal receipts and any delivery proof.
2. **Note the dates** of each notice, the date of possession and the proposed date of sale.
3. **Check the contents**, such as the property description, the amount claimed and the sale details.
4. **Compare the sequence** of events with what the Rules require, using the current text.
5. **Keep your own records** of what you received and when.

Gaps or inconsistencies may be worth raising, but whether they matter depends on the facts of each case.

## Where such issues are raised

A borrower who believes the Rules were not followed can look at a **Section 17 application before the Debts Recovery Tribunal**, filed within the limitation period stated in the Act. The Supreme Court in *United Bank of India v. Satyawati Tondon* (2010) 8 SCC 110 discouraged writ petitions against SARFAESI action where an effective remedy exists, so the statutory route is usually the first place to look.

## Related decisions

*Mardia Chemicals v. Union of India* (2004) 4 SCC 311 recognised that a borrower can object to a notice and that the creditor must reply with reasons. *Transcore v. Union of India* (2008) 1 SCC 125 held that SARFAESI and the RDB Act operate together.

## Practical points

- Treat every notice as important and **keep it safely**.
- Do not assume a procedural step was followed; **verify it against the documents**.
- Act **promptly**, because time limits apply.
- Seek advice on your own papers before deciding what to do.
